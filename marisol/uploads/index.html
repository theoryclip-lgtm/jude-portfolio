IThe Kitchen

The menu is written at six in the morning, on the sand, before we know what we're cooking.

The Newport dory fleet has pulled boats onto the beach at the foot of the pier since 1891. It's the last of its kind on the West Coast. Our chef walks down at dawn, buys what came up, and drives it four blocks to the kitchen. That's the whole sourcing strategy.

Which means we can't tell you in advance what you'll eat. Some nights it's sand dabs and spot prawns. Some nights the swell was wrong and dinner leans on the farm in San Juan Capistrano instead. What we can promise is that nothing on your table sat in a truck overnight.

— Elena Ruiz-Marín, chef

The Progression

Five courses, roughly two hours, moving from cold and raw through fire and finishing sweet. This is last Saturday's menu — yours will differ.

I

Kanpachi, green almond, sea fig

cured ninety minutes, buttermilk, wild fennel pollen

The only course that never changes shape. Something raw, something green, something from the shore.

Poured with
Txakoli, Getaria
II

Spot prawns, head butter, charred lemon

grilled over red oak, brought out whole

Eaten with hands. We bring cloth, not a fork.

Poured with

III

Sand dab, brown butter, preserved fig leaf

bone-in, dressed at the pass

Whatever the fleet had most of that morning lands here. Halibut and white seabass are the usual understudies.

IV

The one course we hold back for the fire. Carved at the table for parties of four or more.

Elena's grandmother's recipe, unchanged since 1968 except for the ice.

IVPrivate & Events

Six seats facing the fire.

The chef's counter runs one seating a night — an extended nine-course version of the progression, served by the cooks who made it. It books out about six weeks ahead, and it's the only table where you get to ask questions all night.

For larger parties the room buys out cleanly at thirty-four, and we'll build the menu around whatever the occasion is.
